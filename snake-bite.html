Snake bite: what to do

Stay calm. Most snake bites are not fatal, and the right first aid and quick hospital care make the difference. Antivenom at a hospital is the only specific treatment.

Bitten? Go to the nearest hospital now. Keep the person still and calm, and take them by the safest transport available. They must not walk far, run, or drive themselves.

First aid

  1. Reassure the person. Most bites are from non-venomous snakes. Panic makes the heart beat faster and spreads venom faster.
  2. Keep them still. Lay them down and keep the bitten limb still, at about heart level if you can.
  3. Immobilise the limb. Splint it along its length, like a broken bone. Do not press on the wound.
  4. Remove rings, watches, bangles and tight clothing near the bite before the area swells.
  5. Note the time of the bite and, only if you can do it safely, a photo of the snake. Do not risk another bite to do this.
  6. Transport urgently to the nearest PHC or hospital. Phone ahead if you can.
  7. Give nothing by mouth (no food, drink, or medicine) until they reach a health centre.
  8. Start CPR if they stop breathing or have no pulse, and keep going until help arrives.

Do not

  • Do not apply a tourniquet or any tight band. It can cut off blood flow and cause tissue damage.
  • Do not cut the wound or try to drain it.
  • Do not suck the venom out, by mouth or any other way.
  • Do not apply ice, heat, or electric shocks.
  • Do not give alcohol, herbs, or traditional remedies. They have no proven benefit.
  • Do not try to kill, catch, or carry the snake. Keep a safe distance.

Signs of venom: tell the doctor

Lack of pain or swelling does not mean the snake was harmless. Some krait bites hurt very little, and signs can start hours later. Everyone bitten should be checked in hospital.

  • Swelling or pain that spreads from the bite
  • Bleeding from the gums, nose, or bite site, or blood in urine, stool, or vomit
  • Drooping eyelids, blurred or double vision
  • Difficulty swallowing, speaking, or breathing
  • Weakness or paralysis, especially of the face, neck or limbs
  • Vomiting, collapse, or confusion
  • Passing little or no urine, or dark urine (possible kidney injury)

At the hospital

From the Health Ministry's Standard Treatment Guidelines: Snakebite (June 2016), with WHO snakebite guidance. Doctors decide the treatment.

  1. Check breathing, circulation and consciousness first. Keep an airway open.
  2. Record the time of the bite, the snake (if known), and the first signs.
  3. Check blood clotting with the 20-minute whole blood clotting test (20WBCT), and repeat it.
  4. Look for neurotoxic signs (drooping eyelids, weakness, breathing trouble), bleeding, and spreading swelling.
  5. Give antivenom if there are signs of venom: neurotoxic signs, bleeding, incoagulable blood, or spreading swelling. Antivenom is the only specific treatment. Doses are set by the doctor.
  6. Keep the limb immobilised. Do not cut the wound.
  7. Watch for allergic reactions to antivenom, and for signs of kidney failure.
  8. Give tetanus protection as needed.

Disclaimer

This page is general guidance only. It is not a substitute for emergency medical care, a doctor's advice, or the local hospital's protocol. If someone has been bitten, get to a hospital immediately. Call your local emergency number if you cannot reach care quickly.
